Brussels Forces Google to Open Android to Competitors' AI

The European Commission has mandated Google to open its Android operating system to third-party artificial intelligence assistants, a decision under the Digital Markets Act (DMA). The ruling, effective by July 2027, requires Google to grant full access to core Android functionalities to competing AI assistants, arguing that current restrictions put European users at a disadvantage compared to Google's Gemini AI. The move aims to level the playing field by allowing users to activate their preferred AI assistants via voice commands, similar to 'Hey Google,' for tasks like booking rides or messaging. The Commission emphasized 'solid safeguards' to protect user privacy and device security. Additionally, Google must share search data with other search engines and chatbots by January 2027, enabling smaller competitors to improve services and offer privacy-focused alternatives. The decision, legally binding but subject to judicial review, faces criticism from Google, which warns of privacy risks from data sharing. European officials stress the importance of fair competition and user choice, ensuring transparency and data protection in the process.

Nasa launches mission to save falling space telescope

NASA has launched a daring mission to rescue the falling Swift observatory, a space telescope critical for studying high-energy cosmic phenomena. The LINK spacecraft, equipped with three robotic arms and advanced guidance systems, aims to intercept the telescope as it descends from its original 373-mile orbit to a perilously low 220-mile altitude. Developed by Katalyst Space Technologies, the mission involves navigating a moving target while accounting for Swift's unpredictable orbital shifts. Once close, LINK will slowly attach to the telescope and use its thrusters to gradually raise its orbit back to stability. The operation, described as "high risk" by experts, represents a groundbreaking attempt to salvage a scientifically invaluable asset. Swift, launched in 2004, has provided unique insights into cosmic explosions caused by dying stars and their remnants. Success could pave the way for future missions, including potential repairs to the Hubble Space Telescope. The rescue effort highlights advancements in autonomous robotics and space engineering, blending cutting-edge technology with critical scientific preservation.
